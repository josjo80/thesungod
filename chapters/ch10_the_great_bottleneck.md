# Chapter 10 — The Great Bottleneck

For roughly the first half of its history, life on Earth was a resounding success and a crashing bore.

The Sun rose and set on a living planet for something like two billion years before anything you would recognize as ambitious appeared. Microbes covered the seas. They learned astonishing chemical tricks — they figured out how to eat sunlight, exhale oxygen, and rust the oceans red — and by any reasonable measure they thrived. But they stayed small. They stayed simple. A bacterium of three billion years ago and a bacterium of today would, under a microscope, be hard to tell apart. For half the age of the Earth, life idled at the curb with the engine running and went nowhere.

Then, once — as far as we can tell, exactly once — something gave. Cells swelled to hundreds of times their former size. They packed their DNA into a nucleus, grew an internal skeleton, and began stockpiling genetic material at a rate their ancestors could never have afforded. Within a geological blink, that new kind of cell would invent sex, then bodies, then nervous systems, then us. Every plant, every animal, every fungus, every reader of this sentence is descended from that single event. Biologists have a name for the cell it produced — the *eukaryote* — and a much less settled understanding of why it took so agonizingly long to arrive, and why, once it did, everything happened at once.

The delay is the interesting part. Life started early on Earth, which suggests, as we saw two chapters ago, that getting life going is not especially hard. But getting life *complex* clearly was. Something held it at a ceiling for two billion years. In fact, we now think there were two ceilings, pressing down from two completely different directions — one made of energy, the other made of information — and the reason the eukaryote is such a hinge in the history of the world is that both ceilings broke at more or less the same moment, and quite possibly for the same reason.

## The first ceiling: a city powered only by its walls

Start with energy, because everything else in this book runs on it.

A living cell is a tiny factory, and like any factory it has to power its machinery. Bacteria generate their power at their outer membrane — the skin of the cell — by pumping charged particles across it, a bit like water held behind a dam. That works beautifully when you're small. But it hides a trap that springs the moment a cell tries to get bigger.

Picture a cell as a city. Everything the city needs to *do* — its industry, its offices, its population — fills its interior, its volume. But under the bacterial scheme, all of its power has to be generated along its outer wall, its surface. And here geometry turns hostile. As you scale a city up, its interior grows far faster than its perimeter. Double the size of the cell and its demands roughly octuple while its power supply merely quadruples. Keep going and you reach a hard wall: a point past which the interior is simply starving, no matter how much food is around, because the perimeter cannot deliver current fast enough to run it. A bacterium that tries to grow large and complex is a metropolis trying to run its entire economy off the streetlights along its border.

The biologist Nick Lane has argued that this bioenergetic squeeze is the single most important reason complex life stalled for so long. The problem is not food and not sunlight — the Sun's budget was always there for the taking. The problem is *architecture*. A cell built on the bacterial plan has a ceiling on how much energy it can bring to bear on each of its genes, and that ceiling caps how elaborate it can ever become. For two billion years, life kept slamming into it.

> **`FOR THE CURIOUS` — Why bigger cells starve**
>
> A cell's energy *demand* scales with its volume, which grows as the cube of its radius (r³). Its energy *supply*, generated across the outer membrane, scales with surface area, which grows only as the square (r²). The ratio of supply to demand therefore falls off as 1/r: the bigger the cell, the less power it can muster per unit of stuff it has to run. This is the same square-cube law that stops land animals from growing to the size of skyscrapers. For a power-generating membrane it sets a ceiling on size and, with it, on the number of genes a cell can afford to keep switched on. Lane and William Martin made the quantitative version of this case in 2010: eukaryotes command thousands of times more energy per gene than bacteria — not because they are cleverer chemists, but because of where they put their power plants.

## The breakthrough: a cell inside a cell

The escape, when it finally came, was not a refinement. It was a merger.

Somewhere around two billion years ago, one microbe swallowed another and, crucially, failed to digest it. The host was an archaeon — a member of life's other great microbial domain. The guest was a bacterium with a talent for wringing energy out of oxygen. Instead of becoming lunch, the guest stayed on as a lodger. It kept dividing inside its host, generation after generation, and it kept paying rent in the only currency that mattered: power.

Over enormous spans of time the lodger dwindled into a specialist. It shed nearly all of its genes, kept the handful needed to run its power stations, and became a permanent internal organ — the *mitochondrion*. And this changed the geometry that had held life captive. A cell with mitochondria no longer generates power at its outer wall alone. It generates power on the folded inner membranes of hundreds or thousands of these organelles, scattered throughout its interior. The city has stopped running on its perimeter streetlights and built power plants on every block. Fold those membranes tightly enough and their combined surface area can dwarf the outer skin of the cell many times over.

The ceiling was gone. A eukaryote could now afford to grow vast, to carry a genome ten or a hundred times larger than any bacterium, and to keep far more genes switched on at once. The energetic tax that had kept life microbial for two billion years had, in a single accident of indigestion, been abolished.

But energy alone doesn't explain everything that followed. Because there was a second ceiling — and this one was made not of watts but of information.

## The second ceiling: a search that got too slow

Here is a puzzle that has nothing obvious to do with power.

Evolution, stripped to its essentials, is a search. It rummages through the space of possible genes, keeps the versions that work, and discards the rest. In the microbial world, that search had a very direct target: proteins. A gene coded for a protein, the protein folded into a molecular machine, and a longer gene meant a longer, more capable machine. For billions of years, as genes grew, the proteins they specified grew right alongside them. Life was getting more capable by getting more elaborate, one amino acid at a time.

And then, at the dawn of the eukaryotes, that lockstep broke. Genes kept growing. Proteins stopped.

This is the striking finding of a 2025 study by Enrique Muro and colleagues, published in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*. They measured the lengths of genes and proteins across more than six thousand species spanning the whole tree of life, and they found a clean signature of a *phase transition* — the same abrupt, threshold-crossing kind of change that turns water to ice. Below a critical gene length, protein length tracks gene length faithfully. Above it, the two decouple: proteins level off at an average of around five hundred amino acids and essentially stop growing, while genes sail on getting longer. That threshold, in their data, marks the birth of the eukaryotic cell.

Why would proteins hit a wall? Because the search for new ones was becoming impossibly slow. The number of ways a chain of amino acids can fold explodes as the chain gets longer, and past a few hundred units, evolution faces a combinatorial haystack so vast that finding a useful new needle takes longer than the time available to find it. By the time a promising large protein turned up, the world it was meant for had already changed. Life had, in effect, explored the searchable space of proteins and hit the far edge of what its methods could reach. This is the informational twin of the energy ceiling: not a shortage of power, but a shortage of *search*.

So what were all those still-lengthening genes doing, if not building bigger proteins? They were building something subtler: control. The extra genetic material was largely *non-coding* — regulatory sequences, splicing machinery, switches that decide when and where each protein gets made. Life stopped trying to invent ever-grander machines and started learning to orchestrate the machines it already had. If DNA is a kind of computer, evolution had stopped writing longer programs and started writing a better operating system — one that could run the old programs in vastly more combinations. Muro's team frame this as an *algorithmic* phase transition: a change not in life's hardware but in its method of search.

A caution worth stating plainly, because this book would rather flag an uncertainty than paper over it. This is a recent and genuinely novel idea, not settled textbook consensus, and it comes with a loose thread: the authors' model dates the transition to about 2.6 billion years ago, somewhat earlier than the roughly 1.6-to-2.1-billion-year window that most fossil and molecular-clock estimates give for the first eukaryotes. Whether that gap reflects a real early origin, a quirk of the model, or the ordinary messiness of reading deep time is not yet clear. Treat the phase-transition story as a compelling hypothesis on the rise, not a closed case.

## Why both ceilings fell together

Now the two threads pull together, and this is the part worth slowing down for, because it is a miniature of the whole argument of this book.

We have two bottlenecks. One is energetic: cells couldn't get big enough to be complex. One is informational: evolution's search couldn't find its way to greater complexity fast enough. Two entirely different problems, in two entirely different currencies — and they were solved at the same moment, in the same cell. That is a suspicious coincidence, and it may not be a coincidence at all.

Here is the link. That new regulatory operating system — the flood of non-coding DNA, the splicing, the switches — is *expensive*. Maintaining and running a genome many times larger than a bacterium's, with whole new layers of control, costs energy the old architecture could never have supplied. The informational breakthrough was only affordable *because* the energetic breakthrough had just happened. And there's a further, elegant twist: much of the room for that new regulatory DNA in the nucleus was opened up as the mitochondria, streamlining themselves, exported their own genes to the host's genome — a housekeeping move that made sense only on energetic grounds. Muro's own paper points, at its close, toward exactly this feedback between energy and information as the deeper story still to be told.

So the eukaryote is not the story of a single lucky break. It is two ceilings falling together, each one's collapse making the other's possible. Energy bought the search; the search spent the energy. A gradient — the Sun's power, now delivered on a thousand interior membranes — was harnessed, and an information ratchet, evolution's slow accumulation of what works, was suddenly free to run at a pace it had never managed before. Gradient and ratchet, the two engines this book keeps returning to, meshing for the first time into something that could genuinely compound.

And compound it did. Freed from both ceilings, cells ballooned in size and intricacy. They took to living in cooperatives, and the first true multicellular bodies followed not long after. In a second act of the same swallowing trick, one lineage engulfed a photosynthesizing bacterium and kept it as an internal solar panel — the chloroplast — giving us the entire green world of plants. The two billion years of stalled microbial life were over. What replaced them was the riot of complexity we still live inside, and belong to.

The bottleneck, in the end, is the most hopeful chapter in the story so far. It tells us that life can sit at a ceiling for an unimaginably long time, going nowhere, looking for all the world like it has finished — and still have its greatest leap ahead of it, waiting on a single change in architecture to let the accumulated pressure through.
